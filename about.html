About Phylosaur

Phylosaur is a free educational dinosaur guessing game built around phylogenetic classification. Instead of answering questions about size, age, or geography, players identify a hidden dinosaur genus by following its position through the dinosaur family tree.

The name combines phylo-, referring to evolutionary lineage, with -saur, the historical element familiar from Dinosauria and many dinosaur names. It also reflects the project's simple purpose: making dinosaur classification approachable through play.

How the dinosaur guessing game works

Every challenge selects a hidden dinosaur genus. After each guess, Phylosaur compares the stored lineage of the guessed genus with the lineage of the target. The game then reveals the deepest named clade shared by both dinosaurs. A guess that follows more of the target's lineage produces a higher proximity score.

This turns each attempt into a clue. A distant guess may only share Dinosauria or one of its broad branches, while a close guess can reveal a much more specific family or subgroup. Hints expose the next part of the target lineage, and the tree records the strongest path found during the game.

  • Daily challenges provide the same target for a selected level during the player's local day.
  • Practice mode allows unlimited games without affecting Daily statistics.
  • Private challenges let friends play the same hidden dinosaur using a shared code.
  • Completed games unlock dinosaur entries in the Museum.

Why phylogenetic classification?

Phylogenetics describes hypotheses about evolutionary relationships. A clade contains a common ancestor and its descendants, and nested clades describe increasingly specific relationships. Phylosaur represents each playable genus as one ordered path beginning with Dinosauria and continuing toward narrower groups.

The game is intended as an introduction to navigating those relationships. Its scores measure agreement between stored lineage paths; they do not measure geological time, anatomical similarity, or a literal percentage of shared evolution.

Difficulty levels

The five levels represent public familiarity rather than scientific importance or phylogenetic complexity. Difficulty is informed by thirty-day Wikipedia pageview totals. Frequently visited genera generally appear in lower levels, while less familiar names tend toward higher levels.

Pageviews are an imperfect proxy. Media attention, language, ambiguous names, and the collection period can affect them. Biological homonyms are reviewed conservatively when traffic may belong to an unrelated plant, animal, person, or place.

Data sources and taxonomic methodology

The catalogue began with open paleontological occurrence and taxonomic records, particularly material from the Paleobiology Database, followed by filtering and manual review. The current lineage database combines earlier curated classifications with lineages retrieved through taxodist from The Taxonomicon.

Automated checks remove placeholder ranks, detect ambiguous names and biological homonyms, prevent playable genera from being used as internal clades, and look for named clades assigned to conflicting parents. When an automated lineage is unresolved or removes too much useful structure, a reviewed fallback may be retained for later investigation.

Museum descriptions are requested from Wikipedia for general orientation. Images come from reusable media identified through Wikimedia Commons or explicitly licensed sources. Available creator, source, and licence information is displayed with each image.

Phylosaur is an independent educational project and should not be treated as a primary scientific source. For formal research, classifications and claims should be checked against the relevant systematic literature.

Scientific limitations

Phylogenetic trees are scientific hypotheses that can change as new fossils, character matrices, and analyses become available. Published studies may recover competing positions or unresolved relationships, while the game must store one practical path for each genus.

Some included names may be historically important, disputed, synonymous, or based on limited material. Detailed placement in the game should therefore not be interpreted as equal certainty at every node. Reports of taxonomic errors and suggestions for improved placements are welcome.

Further resources

Readers interested in dinosaur classification and phylogenetic systematics can consult the Paleobiology Database, primary systematic publications, museum research pages, and specialist references such as The Dinosauria and works on phylogenetic methodology.
