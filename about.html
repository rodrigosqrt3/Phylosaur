About Phylosaur

Phylosaur is an independent educational game about the dinosaur family tree. A hidden genus must be identified by comparing guesses across a branching classification. Each attempt reveals the deepest named clade shared by the guess and the target, turning classification into a process of navigation rather than memorization. Gradually, familiar names become landmarks within a larger evolutionary history.

The name is a deliberate meeting of language and subject. The scientific element phylo-, from Greek phylon (a lineage, tribe, or related group), points to phylogeny: the history and pattern of evolutionary relationships. Its sound also recalls philo-, from Greek philos, associated with affection or love. -saur, from Greek sauros (lizard), is the historical element familiar from Dinosauria and countless dinosaur names.

Phylosaur can therefore be read in two complementary ways: a game about dinosaur phylogeny and a small expression of love for dinosaurs. The resemblance to "lizard" belongs to the history of the word, not to a claim that dinosaurs were merely lizards.

Phylogenetic foundations

Modern biological classification rests upon phylogenetic systematics, a methodology that organizes organisms according to their evolutionary relationships rather than superficial morphological similarities. This approach, pioneered by Willi Hennig in the mid-twentieth century, recognizes that meaningful taxonomic groupings (clades) must be monophyletic, comprising an ancestor and all of its descendants. Each clade is diagnosed by synapomorphies, which are derived character states inherited from a common ancestor and shared exclusively among its descendants.

Within this framework, Dinosauria represents a well-supported clade united by numerous osteological synapomorphies. The group is formally diagnosed by features including an open acetabulum, an elongated deltopectoral crest on the humerus, and modifications to the ankle joint that facilitate a parasagittal gait. Dinosauria has traditionally been divided into Saurischia, characterized by a triradiate pelvis with the pubis directed anteroventrally, and Ornithischia, distinguished by a retroverted pubis and predentary bone. Modern phylogenetic analyses place Avialae (birds) within Saurischia as highly derived theropods, rendering traditional distinctions between "dinosaurs" and "birds" paraphyletic and therefore scientifically obsolete.

"The combination of such characters... will, it is presumed, be deemed sufficient ground for establishing a distinct tribe or sub-order of Saurian Reptiles, for which I would propose the name of Dinosauria."

- Richard Owen (1842). Report on British Fossil Reptiles, Part II. Report of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, 11: 60-204.

Game mechanics and educational objectives

Players are presented with a mystery taxon and infer its phylogenetic position through iterative hypothesis testing. Each guess reveals the deepest stored node shared between the proposed taxon and the target, thereby progressively constraining the phylogenetic search space. A distant guess may reveal only a broad branch, while a close guess can expose a much more specific family or subgroup.

This mechanism mirrors the general process of phylogenetic inference, in which researchers place taxa by testing relationships and identifying close relatives through character analysis and cladistic methodology. The game simplifies that process into an ordered lineage comparison suitable for play.

Hints reveal the next clade in the hidden lineage. If the full stored lineage is already known, remaining hints instead reveal clues about the genus name. The waiting rule between hints gives the player time to use the new information. The game tree records the strongest route reached so far. New genera encountered in completed games are also added to the Museum.

  • Daily challenges provide the same target for a selected level during the player's local day.
  • Practice mode allows unlimited games without affecting Daily statistics.
  • Private challenges let friends play the same hidden dinosaur using a shared code.
  • The game currently has no fixed guess limit.

Difficulty and public familiarity

The game offers five difficulty tiers determined by taxonomic familiarity as measured through thirty-day Wikipedia pageview statistics. This serves as a proxy for public recognition rather than phylogenetic complexity, fossil completeness, or scientific importance. Frequently visited genera tend toward lower levels, while less familiar names tend toward higher levels. Higher levels therefore include taxa with lower pageview counts, generally lesser-known genera that require broader taxonomic knowledge independently of their scientific importance or systematic resolution.

Pageviews retrieved through Wikimedia services are an imperfect measure shaped by language, public interest, media attention, and the collection period. Homonyms require special attention because traffic may refer to a plant, place, person, or unrelated animal. Genera without reliable traffic data are handled conservatively.

Data sources and taxonomic curation

The genus catalogue began with open paleontological occurrence and taxonomic data, particularly records assembled through the Paleobiology Database, followed by filtering and manual review. The present lineage database combines the project's earlier curated classifications with lineages retrieved through taxodist from The Taxonomicon.

The sources are not simply placed side by side. Automated code cleans placeholder ranks, detects ambiguous names and biological homonyms, removes playable genera used as internal clades, and aligns older fallback paths to a shared taxonomic backbone. The hybrid-building process learns commonly supported paths from successfully resolved genera and uses those paths to bring retained lineages into a coherent structure.

Before an output is accepted, the database is checked for repeated nodes, placeholder clades, incorrect lineage depths, playable genera appearing as ancestors, and named clades assigned to more than one parent. When an automatic result is unresolved or collapses too much useful structure, a reviewed lineage can be retained for later investigation. This produces a practical working tree while preserving a record of which placements came from each source.

Museum summaries are requested from Wikipedia for accessible orientation. Illustrations come from reusable files identified through Wikimedia Commons or explicitly licensed Dinopedia pages. Existing reviewed choices are preserved, while new fallback images must pass checks for subject relevance, file type, reusable licensing, attribution, and minimum quality. Creator, source, and licence information accompanies each available image so that visitors can follow it back to its original record.

Mathematical foundations

The proximity measure computed by Phylosaur has a precise mathematical interpretation. Let $L_A$ be the ordered lineage of a guessed genus $A$, and let $L_B$ be the lineage of the hidden target $B$. Both sequences begin at Dinosauria and proceed toward increasingly specific clades. The game counts how many consecutive entries they share before diverging. If their longest common prefix has length $m(A,B)$, then:

$$m(A,B)=\max\left\{k: L_A[i]=L_B[i]\;\text{for every}\;0\le i<k\right\}$$

The final clade inside this common prefix is the deepest named common ancestor represented by the game. The displayed proximity score is the shared-prefix length divided by the depth of the target:

$$s(A,B)=100\times\frac{m(A,B)}{|L_B|}$$

This percentage answers a direct game question: how much of the target's stored lineage has the guess followed successfully? Because the denominator is $|L_B|$, the target's depth, the score need not be symmetric when two genera have lineages of different lengths. Two distinct genera can occasionally share every stored clade and reach 100% proximity; the exact genus name is still required to win.

A related structural separation can be defined by treating every stored parent-child step as one unit:

$$\Delta(A,B)=|L_A|+|L_B|-2m(A,B)$$

This is not the percentage used during play, but it expresses how many stored steps separate the two lineage paths after their divergence. Named clades are not evenly spaced units of time, anatomy, or evolutionary change, so neither $s$ nor $\Delta$ should be interpreted as geological duration or morphological difference.

From an information-theoretic perspective, each guess is a query that reveals the depth and identity of a shared branch. The revealed clade restricts the remaining candidates to genera compatible with that portion of the target path. Hints reduce uncertainty more directly by exposing the next clade or, after the stored lineage is exhausted, a clue about the target name. The waiting rule between hints creates space for the player to use the new information. Dinosaur branches are highly uneven: one clade may contain hundreds of candidates while another contains only a few. Consequently, different guesses can provide very different amounts of information, which is an important part of the strategy.

Difficulty stratification also has a statistical basis. Let $V=\{v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_n\}$ be the sorted vector of usable thirty-day pageview totals. Boundaries near the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles divide the observed distribution into five familiarity bands. These are empirical game-design categories rather than universal measures of difficulty.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Phylogenetic trees are hypotheses of evolutionary relationships, not immutable facts. The topology presented here represents one interpretation of available data and may differ from analyses that use different character matrices, taxon sampling strategies, or analytical methods. Phylogenetic resolution varies across the dinosaurian tree. While some clades such as Tyrannosauridae exhibit robust support across multiple independent analyses, others remain poorly resolved because of incomplete fossil records, morphological conservatism, or competing interpretations. Users should interpret the topology with appropriate epistemic humility, recognizing that scientific understanding of dinosaurian phylogeny continues to evolve.

Taxonomic nomenclature follows standard conventions in which genus names are italicized and capitalized, while higher-level clade names are rendered in standard font with initial capitalization. Some included taxa may represent nomina dubia, names of doubtful validity based on insufficient diagnostic material, or taxonomic wastebasket groups subject to continuing systematic revision.

The game must express each genus as one ordered path, but published analyses may instead recover competing positions or an unresolved polytomy. Synonyms create another difficulty: a historical genus may later be absorbed into another while remaining culturally or historically recognizable. Phylosaur may retain such names when they contribute to the educational catalogue, while unresolved placements are recorded for future review. The resulting tree is intentionally detailed, but its detail should not be confused with equal certainty at every node.

Phylosaur is an independent educational project and should not be treated as a primary scientific source. For formal research, classifications and claims should be checked against the relevant systematic literature.

Acknowledgments and further resources

This project acknowledges the foundational work of paleontologists whose systematic research underlies our current understanding of dinosaurian phylogeny. Readers seeking deeper engagement with the primary literature can consult the Paleobiology Database, which archives stratigraphic and taxonomic occurrence data; the Tree of Life Web Project, which provides broad phylogenetic overviews; Fossilworks, which maintains taxonomic and bibliographic records; museum research pages; and the original systematic publications relevant to individual clades and genera.

Readers interested in formal training in phylogenetic methodology may also consult Phylogenetics: Theory and Practice of Phylogenetic Systematics by E. O. Wiley and Bruce Lieberman, or Inferring Phylogenies by Joseph Felsenstein.
